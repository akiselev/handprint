The lift in the east wing had been out of service for eleven days.

This was a matter of public record, because there was a notice, and a notice is
how a building tells you what is true. Buildings are not good at truth. They are
extremely good at notices.

On the twelfth morning Ellen Marsh pressed the button, which she had done every
morning since the notice went up, out of the same deep human instinct that makes
people push a door marked PULL and then look around to see who noticed. The
doors opened.

She did not get in.

She stood and looked at the lift, and the lift stood and was open, and between
them there passed the sort of silence that happens when neither party is
prepared to go first.

Then she went to find someone to tell, which is what you do with a fact you do
not want to be alone with.

The someone was Robert Ayles, who managed the building and was eating a sandwich
behind a stack of nine forms. He put the sandwich down. There is no unhappier
moment in the working day.

'The lift is working,' Ellen said.

'Which lift.'

'The east one. The one with the notice on it.'

Robert looked at the forms. Nine of them, all about the lift, filled in over
eleven days by four different people who had never met, and the ninth one
unsigned. It was a complete history of an event that had not happened.

'It can't be working,' he said. 'The engineer is coming on Thursday.'

'It opened when I pressed the button.'

'That's not the same as working.'

Ellen said she thought it was fairly close. Robert explained that a lift is
certified as working by an engineer, and that until an engineer has certified
it, the lift is out of service whatever it does when you press the button, and
that this was not his rule.

Nobody's rules are ever theirs. Rules arrive the way weather arrives. Somewhere
there is a man who made the first one, and he has been very careful not to be
found.

They went down to look at it. The notice was taped to the doors at chest height
in the building's official typeface and it said LIFT OUT OF SERVICE — PLEASE USE
STAIRS. Behind the notice the doors were open. The light inside was on.

Robert pressed the button. The doors, which were already open, closed. Then they
opened.

'There,' said Ellen.

'That proves nothing.'

He stepped inside and stood in the middle of the car with his hands at his sides
and his eyes on the ceiling. The doors closed. There was a pause of about four
seconds. Then the lift went down one floor and opened on the basement, where he
had not asked to go and where nobody had pressed anything.

Ellen took the stairs. He was standing exactly where she had left him, which was
now somewhere else.

'It brought you here,' she said.

'I noticed.'

'Why?'

'I don't know.'

They looked at the panel. Seven buttons. None of them lit. Robert pressed the
ground floor and nothing happened, and he pressed it again, and the doors closed
and the lift went up two floors, past the ground floor, and opened on the first,
where a woman with a box was waiting. She got in without looking at either of
them and pressed for the basement.

The lift went to the fourth floor and opened.

'Is this normal,' the woman said.

'The lift is out of service,' said Robert.

The woman looked at the open doors, and the corridor, and the two people
standing in a lift that was out of service. Then she picked up her box and
walked to the stairs, and did not say anything else, and was the only person in
the building that day to act on the evidence.

Robert stayed in the lift for another twenty minutes. Ellen brought him a chair
from the fourth-floor waiting area, which he did not use, and a cup of tea,
which he drank standing up. Every time the doors closed the lift moved.
Every time it moved it went somewhere nobody had chosen. Every time it arrived
it opened and waited and then closed again by itself, unhurried, like something
that had all day and had been given the run of the building.

At eleven o'clock he came out and went upstairs and took the ninth form and
signed it in the box marked ENGINEER, and crossed that out, and wrote his own
name in the box marked REPORTED BY, and put the form at the bottom of the stack
rather than the top.

'What did you write,' Ellen asked.

'That the lift is out of service.'

'It isn't, though.'

'No,' Robert said. 'But that's the form there is.'

He went back to his sandwich. The notice stayed up, and for three days the lift
ran and the east wing walked, because it is easier to climb four flights than to
argue with stationery. On Thursday the engineer came and found nothing wrong and
went away, and the notice came down that afternoon, and the lift stopped an hour
later and stayed stopped for a month.

The building had been told what it was. Buildings listen.
