It is not that I am unwilling to be believed. It is that I have set down, in the
plainest order I can manage, a sequence of events which admits of no
interpretation whatever, and I find that the plainness itself is what nobody can
forgive.

For eleven days the lift in the east wing stood out of service. Upon the twelfth
morning Ellen Marsh pressed the button — she had pressed it every morning of the
eleven, from habit, as one touches a scar — and the doors opened.

She did not enter. Let that be recorded first, and let it be recorded that of
everyone concerned in this business she alone behaved with sense. She stood in
the corridor and regarded the open car, and the open car, lit from within,
returned her regard, and she did not enter.

Then she went to find someone to tell, which is the beginning of the whole
error.

The someone was Robert Ayles, who managed the building, seated at his desk with
a sandwich and with nine forms. He put the sandwich down.

"The lift is working," she said.

"Which lift."

"The east one. The one with the notice on it."

He looked at the forms. There were nine of them, filled out over eleven days by
four persons who had never met, and the ninth was unsigned. I ask the reader to
hold that number in mind. Nine forms; four hands; eleven days; one signature
absent. I have thought about those figures a great deal since.

"It can't be working," he said. "The engineer is coming on Thursday."

"It opened when I pressed the button."

"That's not the same as working."

She said she thought it fairly close, and he explained to her that a lift is
certified as working by an engineer, and that until the engineer has certified
it the lift is out of service regardless of what it does. He said the rule was
not his. Men always say that the rule is not theirs. It is the last thing they
say before they step into the car.

They went down. The notice was taped to the doors at the height of a man's
chest, in the building's official type, LIFT OUT OF SERVICE — PLEASE USE STAIRS,
and behind it the doors stood open, and the light within was burning steadily,
and had been burning, I now believe, for eleven days.

He pressed the button. The doors, which were already open, closed. Then they
opened.

"There," said Ellen.

"That proves nothing."

He stepped inside. He stood at the center of the car with his hands at his sides
and his face lifted to the ceiling, and she watched him from the corridor. The
doors closed. There was a pause — I have it from her that it was four seconds,
and I do not doubt her, but I would give a great deal to know what those four
seconds were for — and then the car descended one floor and opened upon the
basement, where he had not asked to go, and where no hand had called it.

She took the stairs. She found him standing precisely as she had left him.

"It brought you here," she said.

"I noticed."

"Why?"

"I don't know."

They examined the panel. Seven buttons; none lit. He pressed the ground floor;
nothing followed. He pressed it again, and the doors closed, and the car rose
two floors, passing the ground floor without pausing, and opened upon the first,
where a woman stood with a box. She entered without looking at either of them
and pressed for the basement, and the car took the three of them to the fourth
and opened.

"Is this normal," the woman said.

"The lift is out of service," he answered.

She looked at the doors, and at the corridor, and at the two of them standing
beside her, and she took up her box and went to the stairs and said nothing
further; and she is the only one of us who understood what she had been shown.

He remained in the car twenty minutes. Ellen brought him a chair, which he did
not use, and tea, which he drank standing. Each time the doors closed the car
moved. Each time it moved it went where no one had chosen. Each time it arrived
it opened, and held open, and closed again of its own accord — and it is that
last, the closing of its own accord, that I would ask the reader to consider
before dismissing the whole account.

At eleven he came out. He went up, and took the ninth form, and signed it in the
box marked ENGINEER; and struck the signature through; and wrote his own name in
the box marked REPORTED BY; and placed the form at the bottom of the stack.

"What did you write," she asked.

"That the lift is out of service."

"It isn't, though."

"No," he said. "But that's the form there is."

He returned to his sandwich. The notice remained. For three days the car ran and
the east wing walked, obedient to a piece of paper. On Thursday the engineer
came, and found nothing wrong, and went away. The notice came down that
afternoon. Within the hour the lift stopped, and it did not move again for a
month; and I have never been able to satisfy myself that the notice and the
stopping were unconnected, nor to explain to anyone why the question keeps me
awake.
