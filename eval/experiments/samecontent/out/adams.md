The lift in the east wing had been out of service for eleven days, which was
not in itself remarkable. What was remarkable, and what nobody was in a
position to appreciate on the morning of the twelfth day, was that it had spent
those eleven days working perfectly.

Ellen Marsh pressed the button out of habit. She had pressed it every morning
since the notice went up, in the way that people continue to try light switches
in rooms where the power has been off since Tuesday, and this morning the doors
opened.

She did not get in. She stood and looked at the open lift for a while, and the
open lift, as far as anyone could tell, looked back.

Then she went to find someone to tell, which is the second thing people do when
the world stops behaving, the first being to check that nobody saw them notice.

The someone was Robert Ayles, who managed the building and who was eating a
sandwich at a desk with nine forms on it. He put the sandwich down when she came
in, and did it slowly, the way a man does when he suspects he is not going to be
allowed to pick it up again.

"The lift is working," Ellen said.

"Which lift."

"The east one. The one with the notice on it."

Robert looked at the forms. There were nine of them and they were all about the
lift. They had been filled in over eleven days by four different people, none of
whom had met, and the last one had not been signed.

"It can't be working," he said. "The engineer is coming on Thursday."

"It opened when I pressed the button."

"That's not the same as working."

Ellen said that she thought it was fairly close. Robert explained that a lift is
certified as working by an engineer, and that until an engineer has certified
it, the lift is out of service regardless of what it does when you press the
button. He said this was not his rule. He said it in the voice of a man who had
explained it before and expected to explain it again, possibly for ever,
possibly as a career.

They went down to look at it. The notice was still taped to the doors at chest
height, printed in the building's official typeface, which had been chosen in
1987 by a committee and had been quietly resented ever since. It said LIFT OUT
OF SERVICE — PLEASE USE STAIRS. The doors were open behind it. The light inside
was on.

Robert pressed the button. The doors, which were already open, closed. Then they
opened again.

"There," said Ellen.

"That proves nothing."

He stepped inside and stood in the middle of the car with his hands at his sides
and his eyes on the ceiling, and Ellen watched him from the corridor. The doors
closed. There was a pause of about four seconds, during which the lift appeared
to be thinking, and then it went down one floor and stopped and opened onto the
basement, where Robert had not asked to go and where nobody at all had pressed
anything.

Ellen took the stairs. When she arrived he was still standing in the car and the
doors were still open and he had not moved.

"It brought you here," she said.

"I noticed."

"Why?"

"I don't know."

They looked at the panel. There were seven buttons and none of them were lit.
Robert pressed the one for the ground floor. Nothing happened. He pressed it
again, and the doors closed, and the lift went up two floors, past the ground
floor, and opened on the first, where a woman with a box was waiting. She got in
without looking at either of them and pressed the button for the basement.

The lift went to the fourth floor and opened.

"Is this normal," the woman said.

"The lift is out of service," Robert said.

The woman considered the open doors, and the corridor beyond them, and the two
people standing in a lift that was out of service, and she picked up her box and
walked to the stairs and did not say anything else, which was, in the
circumstances, the most sensible contribution anyone made all morning.

Robert stayed in the lift for another twenty minutes. Ellen brought him a chair
from the fourth-floor waiting area, which he did not use, and a cup of tea,
which he drank standing up. Each time the doors closed the lift moved. Each time
it moved it went somewhere nobody had chosen. Each time it arrived the doors
opened and stayed open until they closed again on their own, and there began to
be something in the timing of it that was very slightly worse than menace, which
was patience.

At eleven o'clock he came out and went upstairs and got the ninth form and
signed it in the box marked ENGINEER, and then crossed that out, and wrote his
own name in the box marked REPORTED BY, and put the form at the bottom of the
stack rather than the top.

"What did you write," Ellen asked.

"That the lift is out of service."

"It isn't, though."

"No," Robert said. "But that's the form there is."

He went back to his sandwich. The notice stayed up. For three days the lift ran
and the people in the east wing used the stairs, because the notice said to, and
on Thursday the engineer came and found nothing wrong with it and left. The
notice came down that afternoon. The lift stopped working an hour later and
stayed stopped for a month, and Robert Ayles never once suggested that the two
events were connected, because there was no form for that either.
