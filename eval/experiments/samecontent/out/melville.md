Consider the lift. Consider that we have hung this iron room upon a rope and
taught it to rise, and that having taught it, we no longer look at it, but step
into it as into a habit; and consider further that a habit is only a mystery
that has stopped being examined.

For eleven days the lift in the east wing had been out of service. On the
twelfth morning Ellen Marsh pressed the button as she had pressed it every
morning of those eleven, with the flat unhoping thumb of a woman performing a
rite she no longer believes in, and the doors opened.

She did not enter. She stood before that open box of light and did not enter,
and there is more sense in her standing there than in all the walking that came
after.

Then she went to find a man to tell.

The man was Robert Ayles, keeper of the building, and he was at his desk with a
sandwich and nine forms. He set the sandwich down.

"The lift is working," Ellen said.

"Which lift."

"The east one. The one with the notice on it."

Robert looked at the forms. Nine of them; eleven days; four hands, no two of
which had ever met across a table; and the ninth unsigned. Such is the paper
history of a rope and a box. Whole navies have gone down with less written about
them.

"It can't be working," he said. "The engineer is coming on Thursday."

"It opened when I pressed the button."

"That's not the same as working."

Ellen said she thought it fairly close. And Robert explained to her the doctrine
of the certificate: that a lift is not working when it works, but when a man
with a stamp has said that it works; that the deed is nothing and the attesting
is all. He said it was not his rule. No one has ever claimed a rule. The rules
of this world stand about unfathered, like weather.

They went down. The notice was taped at chest height in the building's own
lettering, LIFT OUT OF SERVICE — PLEASE USE STAIRS, and behind the notice the
doors stood open and the light within was burning, so that the thing announced
its death in print while shining plainly with life.

Robert pressed the button. The doors, being open, closed; and opened.

"There," said Ellen.

"That proves nothing."

He stepped in and stood at the center of the car with his hands at his sides and
his face turned up to the ceiling, in the attitude of a man at prayer or of a
man waiting to be told something. The doors closed. There was a pause of four
seconds. Then the lift went down one floor and opened upon the basement, where
he had not asked to go and where no hand had summoned it.

Ellen took the stairs. She found him standing where he had been left.

"It brought you here," she said.

"I noticed."

"Why?"

"I don't know."

They studied the panel. Seven buttons and not one alight. He pressed the ground
floor: nothing. He pressed again, and the doors closed, and the lift rose two
floors and went past the ground floor as a ship goes past a harbor it has been
signalled into, and opened on the first, where a woman waited with a box. She
came in without looking at them and pressed for the basement, and the lift
carried all three to the fourth and opened.

"Is this normal," said the woman.

"The lift is out of service," Robert said.

She looked at the doors and the corridor and at the two of them standing there,
and she took up her box and went to the stairs and said nothing more; and hers
was the only wisdom shown that morning, for she alone drew the conclusion and
acted upon it.

Robert stayed in the car twenty minutes. Ellen brought a chair he would not sit
in and a tea he drank standing. Each time the doors closed the thing moved.
Each time it moved it went where no one had chosen. Each time it arrived it
opened and held itself open and closed again in its own time, and there is a
word for a machine that answers no summons and keeps its own counsel, and the
word is not broken.

At eleven he came out and went up and took the ninth form and signed it in the
box marked ENGINEER; and crossed it out; and wrote his own name in the box
marked REPORTED BY; and set the form at the bottom of the stack.

"What did you write," Ellen asked.

"That the lift is out of service."

"It isn't, though."

"No," Robert said. "But that's the form there is."

He went back to his sandwich. The notice stayed. For three days the lift ran and
the east wing climbed, because a printed word outranks a moving room. On
Thursday the engineer came and found nothing wrong and departed; the notice came
down that afternoon; and the lift stopped, and stayed stopped for a month. Which
of them was out of service — the lift, or the nine forms, or the four hands that
never met, or the man who signed the ninth and put it at the bottom — I leave to
whoever comes after me with a stamp.
