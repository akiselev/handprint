The lift in the east wing had been out of service for eleven days, which is a
long time for a lift and no time at all for a notice.

On the twelfth morning Ellen Marsh pressed the button out of habit. She had
pressed it every morning since the notice went up, in the way people go on
trying a light switch in a room where the power has been off since Tuesday, and
this time the doors opened.

She did not get in.

She stood and looked at the open lift, and the open lift stood and was open, and
neither of them was willing to make the first move.

Then she went to find someone to tell, which is what you do with a fact you do
not want to be alone with.

The someone was Robert Ayles, who managed the building, and who was eating a
sandwich behind a stack of nine forms. He put the sandwich down slowly, the way
a man does when he suspects he will not be allowed to pick it up again.

'The lift is working,' Ellen said.

'Which lift.'

'The east one. The one with the notice on it.'

Robert looked at the forms. There were nine of them, all about the lift, filled
in across eleven days by four people who had never met, and the ninth was
unsigned. It was a complete documentary history of an event that had not
happened.

'It can't be working,' he said. 'The engineer is coming on Thursday.'

'It opened when I pressed the button.'

'That's not the same as working.'

Ellen said she thought it was fairly close. Robert explained that a lift is
certified as working by an engineer, and that until an engineer has certified
it, the lift is out of service regardless of what it does when you press the
button. He said this was not his rule.

Nobody's rules are ever theirs. Rules arrive the way weather arrives. Somewhere
there is a man who made the first one and he has been extremely careful not to
be found.

They went down to look. The notice was taped to the doors at chest height in the
building's official typeface, which had been chosen in 1987 by a committee and
resented ever since, and it said LIFT OUT OF SERVICE — PLEASE USE STAIRS. Behind
it the doors were open. The light inside was on.

Robert pressed the button. The doors, which were already open, closed. Then they
opened again.

'There,' said Ellen.

'That proves nothing.'

He stepped inside and stood in the middle of the car with his hands at his sides
and his eyes on the ceiling. The doors closed. There was a pause of about four
seconds, during which the lift appeared to be thinking it over, and then it went
down one floor and opened on the basement, where he had not asked to go and
where nobody had pressed anything at all.

Ellen took the stairs. He was standing exactly where she had left him, which was
now somewhere else.

'It brought you here,' she said.

'I noticed.'

'Why?'

'I don't know.'

They looked at the panel. Seven buttons. None of them lit. Robert pressed the
ground floor and nothing happened, and he pressed it again, and the doors closed
and the lift went up two floors, past the ground floor, and opened on the first,
where a woman with a box was waiting. She got in without looking at either of
them and pressed for the basement.

The lift went to the fourth floor and opened.

'Is this normal,' the woman said.

'The lift is out of service,' said Robert.

The woman looked at the open doors, and at the corridor beyond them, and at the
two people standing in a lift that was out of service, and then she picked up
her box and walked to the stairs and said nothing else, which was the only
contribution anyone made all morning that could be defended afterwards.

Robert stayed in the lift for another twenty minutes. Ellen brought him a chair
from the fourth-floor waiting area, which he did not use, and a cup of tea,
which he drank standing up. Every time the doors closed the lift moved. Every
time it moved it went somewhere nobody had chosen. Every time it arrived it
opened and waited and closed again in its own time, unhurried, and there began
to be something in the timing that was very slightly worse than menace, which
was patience.

At eleven o'clock he came out, went upstairs, took the ninth form, and signed it
in the box marked ENGINEER, and crossed that out, and wrote his own name in the
box marked REPORTED BY, and put the form at the bottom of the stack rather than
the top.

'What did you write,' Ellen asked.

'That the lift is out of service.'

'It isn't, though.'

'No,' Robert said. 'But that's the form there is.'

He went back to his sandwich. The notice stayed up, and for three days the lift
ran and the east wing walked, because it is easier to climb four flights than to
argue with stationery. On Thursday the engineer came and found nothing wrong and
left, and the notice came down that afternoon, and the lift stopped an hour
later and stayed stopped for a month.

Robert never once suggested the two events were connected. There was no form for
it, and the building had been told what it was, and buildings listen.
