I have a theory about lifts, which I have never been able to get anyone to
listen to, and which I shall therefore set down here, where it can be ignored at
leisure.

The lift in the east wing had been out of service for eleven days. On the
twelfth morning it was working again, and Ellen Marsh discovered this by
pressing the button, which she had gone on pressing every morning since the
notice went up, in the same spirit in which a man goes on rattling a locked door
long after he has satisfied himself that it is locked.

She did not get in. She stood and looked at it. Then she went to find somebody
to tell, which took her eleven minutes, this being a building in which the
finding of somebody to tell is generally the longest part of any business.

The somebody was Robert Ayles, who managed the place, and who was eating a
sandwich with nine forms in front of him. He put the sandwich down when she came
in. I have always thought there is no sadder sight in an office than a man
putting down a sandwich.

"The lift is working," Ellen said.

"Which lift."

"The east one. The one with the notice on it."

Robert looked at the forms. There were nine of them, all concerning the lift.
They had been filled in over eleven days by four different people, and the last
of them had not been signed, which is the natural condition of the ninth form in
any series of nine.

"It can't be working," he said. "The engineer is coming on Thursday."

"It opened when I pressed the button."

"That's not the same as working."

Ellen said she thought it was fairly close. Robert then explained — and I am
bound to say he explained it thoroughly, and at a length that did credit to his
convictions — that a lift is certified as working by an engineer, and that until
an engineer has certified it, the lift is out of service whatever it may do when
you press the button. He added that this was not his rule. Nobody ever does add
that a rule is theirs. In all my experience of rules I have never yet met the
man who made one.

They went down together to look. The notice was still taped to the doors at
chest height and it said LIFT OUT OF SERVICE — PLEASE USE STAIRS. The doors were
open behind it, and the light inside was on, and the whole arrangement had the
air of a shop that has hung up CLOSED and gone on serving.

Robert pressed the button. The doors, which were open, closed; and then they
opened.

"There," said Ellen.

"That proves nothing."

He stepped inside and stood in the middle with his hands at his sides, looking
up at the ceiling, and Ellen watched him from the corridor. The doors closed.
There was a pause of about four seconds. Then the lift went down one floor and
opened on the basement, where he had not asked to go, and where nobody had
pressed anything at all.

Ellen took the stairs. She found him standing exactly as she had left him, in an
open lift, in a basement.

"It brought you here," she said.

"I noticed."

"Why?"

"I don't know."

They examined the panel. Seven buttons, none of them lit. Robert pressed the
ground floor. Nothing. He pressed it again, and the doors closed, and the lift
went up two floors, past the ground floor, and opened on the first, where a
woman with a box was waiting. She got in without looking at either of them and
pressed the button for the basement, and the lift took her to the fourth.

"Is this normal," the woman said.

"The lift is out of service," Robert said.

She looked at the open doors, and at the corridor, and at the two people
standing in a lift that was out of service, and she picked up her box and went
and found the stairs. I have often thought that the people who get out are the
only ones who ever really understand anything.

Robert stayed in for twenty minutes more. Ellen brought him a chair from the
fourth-floor waiting area, which he did not use, and a cup of tea, which he
drank standing up, and I cannot improve upon that as a picture of the English at
work. Each time the doors closed the lift went somewhere nobody had chosen, and
each time it arrived it opened and waited, quite patiently, until it had decided
to close again.

At eleven he came out, went upstairs, took the ninth form, and signed it in the
box marked ENGINEER; and then crossed that out; and wrote his own name in the
box marked REPORTED BY; and then put the form at the bottom of the stack instead
of the top, which is where forms of that kind go.

"What did you write," Ellen asked.

"That the lift is out of service."

"It isn't, though."

"No," said Robert. "But that's the form there is."

Then he went back to his sandwich, which was, I need hardly say, no longer worth
going back to. The notice stayed up. For three days the lift ran and everybody
in the east wing walked, because the notice told them to; and on Thursday the
engineer came and found nothing wrong and went away again; and the notice came
down that afternoon; and the lift then stopped working and remained stopped for
a month. Which is my theory about lifts, and I should be glad if somebody would
tell me where it is wrong.
