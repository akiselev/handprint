It is a truth not universally acknowledged, though it ought to be, that a
building in possession of a good notice must be in want of nothing further.

The lift in the east wing had been out of service eleven days; and on the
twelfth morning, Miss Ellen Marsh, pressing the button from habit rather than
from any expectation of consequence, was surprised by the doors opening.

She did not go in. She stood a little while and considered the lift; and having
considered it, she determined that the proper course was to inform somebody,
which is what one does when one has been surprised and does not wish to be
surprised alone.

Mr. Ayles, who managed the building, was at his desk with a sandwich and with
nine forms; and he put down the sandwich upon her entrance, which she was
disposed to think civil.

"The lift is working," said Ellen.

"Which lift."

"The east one. The one with the notice upon it."

Mr. Ayles looked at his forms. There were nine, all of them relating to the
lift, filled up across eleven days by four persons who had never once been in a
room together; and the last of them was unsigned.

"It cannot be working," said he. "The engineer comes Thursday."

"It opened when I pressed the button."

"That is not the same thing as working."

Ellen replied that she thought it very nearly the same thing. Mr. Ayles then
explained, with that particular patience which is indistinguishable from
reproach, that a lift is certified as working by an engineer, and that until an
engineer has certified it, it is out of service, whatever it may choose to do
when a button is pressed. He was careful to add that the rule was not his own.
It is a great comfort to a man to be governed by a rule he did not make, and Mr.
Ayles took as much comfort from it as the situation allowed.

They went down together. The notice remained taped at the height of the chest,
in the building's official character, and it said LIFT OUT OF SERVICE — PLEASE
USE STAIRS; and behind it the doors stood open, and within, the light burned.

Mr. Ayles pressed the button. The doors, being open, closed; and then opened.

"There," said Ellen.

"That proves nothing."

He stepped in, and stood in the middle of the car with his hands at his sides
and his eyes upon the ceiling, and Ellen observed him from the corridor. The
doors closed; there was a pause of four seconds; and the lift descended one
floor and opened upon the basement, whither he had not desired to go, and
whither nobody had directed it.

Ellen took the stairs. She found him standing where he had stood, in an open
lift, in a basement, with the air of a man who has been carried somewhere and
does not intend to make a remark about it.

"It brought you here," said she.

"I noticed."

"Why?"

"I do not know."

They examined the panel together. Seven buttons; not one of them lit. Mr. Ayles
pressed the ground floor, and nothing followed; he pressed it again, and the
doors closed, and the lift rose two floors, passing the ground floor entirely,
and opened upon the first, where a woman with a box was waiting. She came in
without acknowledging either of them and pressed for the basement; and the lift
carried the three of them to the fourth, and opened.

"Is this normal," said the woman.

"The lift is out of service," said Mr. Ayles.

She looked at the doors, and at the corridor, and at the two persons standing
with her in a lift which was out of service; and she took up her box and went to
the stairs, and said nothing whatever; and of the whole party she was the only
one whose conduct could be entirely defended.

Mr. Ayles remained twenty minutes more. Ellen fetched him a chair from the
fourth-floor waiting area, which he did not sit in, and a cup of tea, which he
drank standing; and every time the doors closed the lift went somewhere nobody
had chosen, and every time it arrived it opened and waited until it was ready to
close again.

At eleven he came out, and went up, and took the ninth form, and signed it in
the box marked ENGINEER; and then struck that through; and wrote his own name in
the box marked REPORTED BY; and laid the form at the bottom of the stack, and
not the top.

"What did you write," Ellen asked.

"That the lift is out of service."

"But it is not."

"No," said Mr. Ayles. "But that is the form there is."

He returned to his sandwich. The notice was not disturbed. For three days the
lift ran, and the east wing walked, the notice having desired them to; and on
Thursday the engineer attended, and found nothing amiss, and withdrew; and the
notice came down in the afternoon; and the lift stopped, and remained stopped a
month. Ellen thought of mentioning the coincidence to Mr. Ayles, and did not,
having by then a tolerably clear idea of what would be required of her in
writing.
